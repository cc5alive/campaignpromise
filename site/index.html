How we know

This prototype uses only government records. Anyone can download the same files and get the same numbers.

Members

Names, party, state, chamber, gender, years served and FEC candidate IDs come from the public-domain congress-legislators dataset, current members file, downloaded Nov 13, 2026. Years served counts time in either chamber up to today.

Money

All money figures cover the 2025–26 cycle and come from FEC bulk files last updated Nov 8, 2026:

  • Direct contributions from a group's PAC: file itpas2, transaction type 24K, net of refunds. A negative net means the money was returned, and the site shows it as "refunded."
  • Earmarked contributions: money from individuals that a group's PAC routed to a campaign. File itcont, transaction type 15E, where the conduit is the group's PAC, as reported by the member's own campaign committee.
  • Outside spending for or against: independent expenditures by the group's super PACs, file itpas2, types 24E (for) and 24A (against).
  • Lines the filer marked as memo entries are left out, so nothing is counted twice.

What is not counted yet

  • Money sent to joint fundraising committees, leadership PACs or caucus PACs rather than the member's own campaign.
  • Individual donors who give on their own without a conduit, even if they are members of a group.
  • Spending reported after the FEC's last bulk update.

Receipts

Each member's page links to FEC search pages for every figure. One known difference: a group's own report of money it forwarded can show more line items than the campaign's report of what it received, because of timing and refunds. Next week we add a side-by-side check of both reports.

Who decides: Claude Opus 5.5

Every promise decision on this site, each approval and each rejection, is made by Claude Opus 5.5, an AI model made by Anthropic. This is a deliberate choice: one consistent reviewer applying published rules, with every call open to inspection. No person approves, rejects or edits these decisions by hand. Corrections are decided the same way, against the same rules, and logged on the Corrections page.

AI can make mistakes. That is why every quote links to its archived source, every vote links to the official roll call, and every decision, including the 17 rejected drafts, is published with its reason in published.json.

Promises and votes

  1. Archive. Campaign websites as they stood in fall 2024 (August to Election Day) are saved from the Internet Archive's Wayback Machine. For most members that is their 2024 campaign; for senators not up in 2024, it is their campaign site as it stood then.
  2. Select. Claude Opus 5.5 reads the saved pages and picks sentences that state a commitment ("Pledge") or a position ("Stated position").
  3. Verify. A script confirms each quote appears word for word in the archived page. Quotes that don't are dropped.
  4. Match votes. Votes come from official House Clerk and Senate roll-call files. Claude Opus 5.5 picks votes that directly test each promise and decides which position keeps it.
  5. Decide. Each draft is approved, shown but not scored (when a vote is related but a kept-or-broken call would rest on a contested reading), or rejected.

So far: 512 drafts for 236 members. 399 approved, 96 shown but not scored, 17 rejected. Of the approved promises, 381 have been tested by a vote: 373 aligned, 7 not aligned, 1 mixed.

Most promises tested so far are party positions tested by party-line votes, so most come out aligned. Specific, local or bipartisan promises are harder to match to a floor vote and are more often shown but not scored.

Why most scored members are Republicans so far. Republicans control both chambers, so most bills that reached a floor vote in 2025–26 were Republican priorities: tax cuts, border and ICE funding, spending cuts, abortion limits. Republican promises on those subjects can each be tested by a different vote. Many Democratic promises, such as protecting abortion rights or passing immigration reform, have had no floor vote yet, and several Democratic promises point to the same bill (H.R. 1), which counts only once. This reflects which votes exist, not a choice of whom to score. We are adding more votes, including Senate amendment votes, to test promises on both sides.
